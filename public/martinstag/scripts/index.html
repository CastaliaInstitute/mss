Martin the Porch Goose: Stories & Chase Scripts

A family storytelling adaptation for November 10–11. The cloak and pine-tree episodes appear in Sulpicius Severus’s Life of Saint Martin; the goose story is a later folk legend. Dialogue here is adapted for family storytelling, not quoted from those sources. Martin the Porch Goose and this chase are our family’s playful additions.

Host preparation

Gather turnips, battery-powered lights, sturdy lantern handles, pine cones, paper clue cards, string, cloaks, goose calls, and Martin the Porch Goose. Adults hollow and carve the turnips; children choose faces, decorate, and help add the lights. After the children collect pine cones on November 10, keep several for the clues. Set out the story props: two cloth pieces for the cloak, a paper pine tree, and goose calls.

After bedtime, move Martin to the final hiding place. Attach the clue cards below to pine cones. Adapt the locations to your home: porch → coat hooks → reading corner → table → lantern station → Martin’s hiding place. Keep the final location secret. Use an adult to lead the route.

November 10 · Morning turnip lanterns

Adults hollow and carve the turnips. Children choose faces, decorate, and help fit battery-powered lights. Attach sturdy handles for the dusk walk.

Host: “Today we’re turning humble turnips into lanterns! What face will yours have? Tonight we’ll carry their warm glow together.”

November 10 · Afternoon pine-cone collecting

Host: “Let’s collect pine cones to bring home. Look closely: each one is a little treasure of autumn. Perhaps they’ll have a story to tell us tomorrow!”

November 10 · Stories with Martin

Set Martin the Porch Goose beside the storyteller. Gather after the lantern walk with warm cider and treats. One adult may read all parts, or share the roles. Allow about 10–15 minutes for all three stories and conversation.

Host: “Welcome, Martin the Porch Goose! Tonight we have three stories about Saint Martin: a cloak shared in the cold, a falling pine tree, and some very noisy geese.”

Story 1 · The Shared Cloak

About 3 minutes. Roles: Narrator, Martin, Traveler, Christ. Props: two pieces of cloth draped together as one cloak. Separate them to show the sharing.

Narrator: “It was winter at the gate of Amiens. The wind blew through the streets, and people hurried home. Martin, a young soldier, came riding toward the gate.”

Traveler: “Please, can anyone help? I have nothing to keep me warm.”

Narrator: “People passed by. Martin stopped. He had already given away what he could spare. Now he had his soldier’s clothing and one warm cloak.”

Martin: “I cannot give you everything you need. But I can share what I have.”

Martin separates the two cloth pieces and places one around the Traveler’s shoulders.

Narrator: “Martin divided his cloak and gave half to the traveler. Some people laughed at the soldier in his shortened cloak. Others wished they had stopped to help.”

Traveler: “Thank you. The wind feels less bitter now.”

Narrator: “That night, Martin dreamed. In the dream, Christ stood before him wearing the very piece of cloak Martin had given away.”

Christ: “Martin, though still preparing for baptism, has clothed me.”

Narrator: “When Martin woke, he remembered the stranger at the gate. He had met someone in need and shared his warmth. That act became the story people remembered.”

Host: “What do we have that we could share? A warm coat? A meal? Our time? Tomorrow’s cloaks will remind us of Martin’s gift.”

Martin the Porch Goose: “Honk! I have feathers to keep me warm. But I can share my company!”

Story 2 · Martin and the Pagan Pine Tree

About 3–4 minutes. Roles: Narrator, Martin, Villager, Crowd. Props: a pine cone and a paper tree. A narrator can tilt the paper tree to show the fall.

Context for the host: This miracle story comes from Sulpicius Severus’s Life of Saint Martin, chapter 13. It reflects religious conflict in late Roman Gaul. The villagers regarded the tree as sacred; tell their part with dignity. The dialogue is a family adaptation.

Narrator: “When Martin was a bishop, he traveled through the countryside preaching his Christian faith. In one village stood an old pine tree beside a pagan shrine. The tree was sacred to the villagers, and they did not want it cut down.”

Villager: “This tree matters to our people. We will not let you fell it.”

Martin: “I believe that God made the trees, and that we should worship God rather than a tree.”

Narrator: “They disagreed. Then one villager proposed a dangerous test.”

Villager: “If you trust your God, stand where this tree will fall. We will cut it down ourselves.”

Narrator: “According to the story, Martin agreed. They placed him on the side toward which the tree leaned and began to cut. His companions watched in fear.”

Crowd: “The tree is moving! It is falling!”

Slowly tilt the paper tree toward Martin. Pause.

Narrator: “As the pine began to fall toward him, Martin raised his hand and made the sign of the cross.”

Martin raises a hand. Tilt the paper tree away from him instead.

Narrator: “The old account says the tree suddenly turned the other way and fell away from Martin. The people were astonished. Many, the writer tells us, came to share Martin’s faith.”

Host: “That is how the Christian writer told the miracle. We can also remember that the villagers loved their sacred tree. How can we speak about things we believe deeply while listening to what matters to someone else?”

Pass around a pine cone from the afternoon’s walk.

Martin the Porch Goose: “A whole pine tree is much too big for my porch! But these little pine cones may come in handy.”

Story 3 · The Geese Who Gave Martin Away

About 3 minutes. Roles: Narrator, Martin, Townsperson, Geese. Children can play the geese with their voices or goose calls. This is a later folk legend about Martin becoming bishop of Tours; versions differ.

Narrator: “Martin liked a simple life of prayer and service. But the people of Tours wanted him to become their bishop.”

Townsperson: “Martin cares for people. Let us ask him to lead us!”

Martin: “A bishop? Me? I would rather stay out of sight.”

Narrator: “So, in this old folk story, Martin slipped away and hid in a goose shelter.”

Martin crouches behind a chair or cloth. The geese notice their visitor.

Martin: “Please, my feathered friends, just a little quiet.”

Geese: “Honk! Honk! HONK!”

Narrator: “The geese did not understand why a person had come into their home. They flapped and called so loudly that everyone could hear.”

Townsperson: “Listen! What is happening over there?”

Geese: “HONK! HONK!”

Narrator: “The people followed the noise and found Martin. His hiding place was a secret no longer. In time, Martin became bishop of Tours.”

Host: “And that is one story connecting Saint Martin with geese. In some places, roast goose also became part of the St. Martin’s feast.”

Martin the Porch Goose: “Honk? Roast goose? I hope nobody means ME!”

Host: “Tonight you are our storytelling guest, Martin. Come close and warm your feet.”

Martin looks around suspiciously. Let the children give one final chorus of goose calls. End with a lantern song and wish Martin goodnight. His disappearance begins tomorrow morning’s Porch Goose adventure.

November 11 · Gifts of cloaks

Host: “Yesterday we heard how Martin shared his cloak. Today these cloaks are gifts for you. May they remind you to share warmth and kindness wherever you go.”

Give the children their cloaks and goose calls, then go to the porch.

Host: “Where is Martin? His place is empty! There’s a pine cone here with a note attached. Let’s read it.”

Martin’s goodbye note

“Dear friends, I heard about the goose feast and became afraid I might be eaten! I’ve gone into hiding. I’ve left clues on your pine cones. Put on your cloaks, sound your goose calls, and follow my trail. Honk, honk! — Martin the Porch Goose”

Printable pine-cone clue cards

Put the first card on the porch. Place each following card at the location named by the previous clue. Hide Martin near the lantern station for the last discovery.

  1. Porch → coat hooks: “Before you follow my feathered tracks, find where coats hang when you come back.”
  2. Coat hooks → reading corner: “Honk your call, then take a look where you curl up with a favorite book.”
  3. Reading corner → table: “Stories are lovely, but where do we meet to share warm drinks and something to eat?”
  4. Table → lantern station: “Yesterday your lights shone bright. Find where your lanterns rest from the night.”
  5. Lantern station → Martin: “I’m hiding nearby! Call softly, look low. Follow the final pine cone and see where I go.”

The reunion

Have the children sound their goose calls as Martin emerges.

Martin: “You found me! Are you here to eat me—or greet me?”

Children: “Greet you!”

Host: “Martin the Porch Goose is our friend. There is a place for you with us.”

Martin: “Then I’ll come home! Honk, honk!”

Return together for a warm drink and a snack. Invite each child to name one kind thing they can do in their new cloak.
